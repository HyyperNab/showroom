1. The Eight Neural Signatures

Brainstem damage from stroke, trauma, or hypoxia alters the respiratory oscillator. These alterations fall into eight recognizable signal patterns[1]. Because each pattern indicates a different biological state, a uniform pneumatic algorithm is often counterproductive. The edge AI classifies the pattern signature first, then prescribes the algorithmic intervention.

2. System Dynamics and Control Logic

State-dependent actuation. A waveform optimized for one neural state may harm another. For example, treating Cheyne-Stokes respiration as central apnea exacerbates the system’s cycling. The architecture requires high-confidence pattern validation before altering ventilation parameters. This approach builds mathematically on recent proofs that the ventral respiratory column operates as a rotational latent manifold[2].

Targeting the biological controller. The lung is a passive mechanical plant governed by well-understood Newtonian mechanics. In neurogenic respiratory failure, the lung functions normally, but the upstream biological controller (the brainstem) fails. Current ventilators adjust the plant. This architecture targets the controller, leaving the established lung mechanics untouched.

Neuromuscular preservation. Ventilator-induced diaphragm dysfunction occurs rapidly during inactivity. By applying non-invasive phrenic stimulation, the system creates a bio-feedback loop that preserves muscle contraction patterns without requiring surgical implants or central venous catheters.

The primary structural innovation is the pacing belt. Current phrenic pacing requires surgical implants or intramuscular electrodes. 4. AI Architecture and Deterministic Safety

Interventions require both pattern classification and strict statistical confidence. The entire pipeline runs on the edge device, ensuring patient health data remains strictly local and simplifying data-privacy compliance by design.

Cyber-physical separation. The AI stack (classification, control, orchestration) acts as the advisory layer. The hardware-level safety floor enforces the limits. Written as a deterministic state machine, the safety floor maintains hard limits on pressure, volume, and rate. If a hardware or software fault occurs, it isolates the AI and reverts the system to conventional ventilation in under 50 milliseconds.

Distributed Machine Learning. To improve the model without transferring raw patient data, the architecture utilizes federated learning[5]. Each hospital site trains the model locally; only the algorithmic gradients are aggregated centrally, secured via differential privacy. During clinical trials, however, the classifier remains strictly frozen to ensure regulatory consistency.

5. Regulatory Strategy for SaMD

In Germany, the IPReG statute[8] mandates an objective assessment of weaning potential before discharging patients to long-term home ventilation. Currently, no automated system meets this requirement. This Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) architecture is designed to fulfill that statutory mandate and address the associated €119,565 per-patient economic burden of weaning failure[4].

Under the EU AI Act[6], this is classified as a high-risk system. Compliance measures — algorithmic risk management, model transparency, human-in-the-loop oversight, and post-market monitoring — are baked into the software architecture from the outset. The validation pathway is strictly gated: starting with retrospective data validation, progressing to a clinical pilot, and concluding with a 500-patient randomized controlled trial. Future algorithmic iterations will leverage predetermined change control plans (PCCP)[7] to enable continuous integration without complete regulatory resubmission.

6. System Limitations and Clinical Unknowns

Training Data Gap. The eight-pattern taxonomy is based on established clinical observation, and the underlying neural models are scientifically validated. However, real-time closed-loop control based on these classifications has not yet been proven to improve weaning outcomes in human trials. The retrospective study is engineered to test this premise on existing datasets before significant capital is deployed.

Hardware Scaling. Transcutaneous stimulation requires higher current than implanted electrodes. It remains an engineering challenge to ensure that a current high enough to elicit meaningful diaphragm contraction is consistently well-tolerated by critically ill patients, accounting for variable skin impedance and clinical edema.

Classification Boundaries. Biological breathing patterns are rarely discrete; a patient may transition from Cheyne-Stokes to central apnea dynamically. The ML classifier outputs a probability distribution to handle this noise, but clinical acceptance of probabilistic UI outputs over binary labels requires targeted usability engineering.

7. Platform & Digital Biomarker Extensions

Digital Biomarkers for Neuro-Monitoring. The edge inference pipeline can function independently as a continuous neural-state monitor. Patients who wean successfully but fail to recover healthy biological variability (e.g., normal Hurst exponents) remain at high risk for re-intubation. A standalone software monitor could serve subacute units without needing the pneumatic ventilator hardware.

IoT Home Care Integration. The sensing belt and edge compute module could support long-term home ventilation patients, feeding the federated learning network and improving remote compliance monitoring outside the intensive care unit.

Predictive Pharmacological Modeling. Because the system reads the neural oscillator’s output, it can detect respiratory changes induced by drugs — such as opioid depression or sedative blunting — before they manifest as critical blood gas abnormalities. This creates a novel early-warning digital biomarker for respiratory pharmacology.
