Sustained participation and quality of life after three decades of phrenic nerve stimulation and home mechanical ventilation in high cervical spinal cord injury: a clinical case report

Laura Peiler1+ · Nabil Ktari2+ · Maria-Cristina Hallwachs3,4 · Sven Hirschfeld4,5 · Martin Groß1,4,6

1 Univ. Oldenburg · 2 Ev. KH Oldenburg · 3 FGQ Stuttgart · 4 DIGAB · 5 BG Klinikum Hamburg · 6 MEDIAN Klinik Bad Tennstedt
+ equal contributions

ABSTRACT

A woman sustained C1–C3, AIS A tetraplegia from a diving accident at age 18. Tracheostomy and continuous mechanical ventilation were implemented. Bilateral phrenic nerve stimulator implantation at one year post-injury permitted daytime ventilator-free breathing and physiological speech. Following 15 months of inpatient treatment, she was discharged home with 24-hour intensive nursing care and has never lived in an institutional setting. Now aged 50, she has held leadership positions in national organizations promoting the rights of people with disability, chairs a charitable foundation, and travels internationally. She reported high life satisfaction and described her work as both exhausting and meaningful.

KEY FINDINGS

01

Complete high cervical tetraplegia with ventilator dependence was compatible with three decades of community living, professional leadership, and reported well-being.

02

Phrenic nerve stimulation enabled ventilator-free breathing and physiological speech, facilitating social interaction and professional communication for over 30 years.

03

Quality of life was primarily associated with psychosocial factors (perceived control, meaningful occupation, social support) — not with injury severity.

04

The patient implemented a Voice-User Interface (VUI) and Ambient Assisted Living (AAL) layer for autonomous environmental control — a real-world case of technology-enabled independence.

RELEVANCE TO TELEMEDICINE

This case demonstrates that long-term technology-supported home care — the exact paradigm TeleClinic is scaling — can enable extraordinary patient outcomes. The patient's voice-controlled environment, remote monitoring needs, and 31-year community-based trajectory are a proof-of-concept for what telemedicine-enabled care can achieve when architected correctly.
